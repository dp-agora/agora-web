On August 27, 2026, the Office of Foreign Assets Control ("OFAC") of the U.S. Department of the Treasury simultaneously amended eight General Licenses under the [Venezuela Sanctions Regulations](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-V/part-591) (31 CFR part 591): GLs 46D, 47B, 48C, 50C, 51C, 52B, 54B, and 61A. OFAC signed all eight on the same day, and each one replaces its prior version in its entirety. The amendment removed —with identical language across the series— the condition that contracts with the Government of Venezuela, PdVSA, or Minerven be construed in accordance with the law of a state of the United States. The dispute resolution forum did not change: It remains the United States, the United Kingdom, France, or Singapore. OFAC explained why in new [FAQ 1267](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/1267): It amended the licenses "in response to investment reforms undertaken by the Government of Venezuela since January 2026." So far as our review reaches, this is the first time OFAC has expressly grounded a relaxation of the Venezuela program on a change in Venezuelan legislation.

## At a Glance

- **What changed.** As of August 27, 2026, eight Venezuela General Licenses no longer require contracts with the Government of Venezuela, PdVSA, or Minerven to be construed under the law of a state of the United States.
- **What remains.** The forum clause: Disputes must be resolved in the United States, the United Kingdom, France, or Singapore.
- **Why it matters.** For the first time, OFAC grounds a relaxation of the Venezuela program in the investment reforms adopted by the Venezuelan Government since January 2026.
- **What to review.** The governing-law clause of every contract drafted under the March or the June standard: It is no longer imposed by the license, and the Venezuelan counterparty may ask to renegotiate it.

## The 2026 Regulatory Sequence

The action continues the sequence OFAC has deployed throughout 2026, which we have analyzed in prior installments:

- **March.** Four licenses defined the sectoral scope of the new framework —hydrocarbons, petrochemicals, electricity, and minerals— (see José P. Barnola Jr., [Four New OFAC General Licenses: The New Framework for Doing Business with Venezuela](/insights/ofac-general-licenses-venezuela-march-2026)).
- **April.** Two licenses enabled the negotiation of contingent contracts and payment channels (see José P. Barnola Jr. and Raúl Sancristóbal, [Two New OFAC General Licenses: Contingent Contracts and Financial Services with Venezuela](/insights/ofac-general-licenses-venezuela-april-2026)).
- **May.** One license opened the professional advisory layer for a potential restructuring of Venezuelan debt (see José P. Barnola Jr. and Raúl Sancristóbal, [OFAC's New General License: Advisory Services for a Potential Restructuring of Venezuelan Debt](/insights/ofac-general-license-58-venezuela-debt-restructuring-advisory-services)).
- **June 10.** Seven energy and mining licenses standardized their contractual clauses: The requirement that U.S. law "govern" the contract became a clause requiring the terms to be "construed" under the law of a state of the United States (see José P. Barnola Jr. and Raúl Sancristóbal, [OFAC Amended Seven Venezuela General Licenses and Standardized Their Contractual Framework](/insights/ofac-amended-seven-venezuela-general-licenses-june-2026)).
- **August 21.** OFAC [issued GL 61](https://ofac.treasury.gov/recent-actions/20260821), on telecommunications, with that same clause.
- **August 27.** The U.S.-law requirement disappears from all eight licenses, including GL 61, six days after it was issued.

## The Eight Amended Licenses

The affected licenses are the seven from June plus the telecommunications license: (i) [GL 46D](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/936806/download?inline) (Venezuelan-origin oil and petrochemical products), (ii) [GL 47B](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/936811/download?inline) (U.S.-origin diluents), (iii) [GL 48C](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/936816/download?inline) (goods and services for hydrocarbons, petrochemicals, and electricity), (iv) [GL 50C](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/936821/download?inline) (operations of the six entities listed in its Annex: BP, Chevron, Eni, Maurel & Prom, Repsol, and Shell), (v) [GL 51C](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/936826/download?inline) (Venezuelan-origin minerals, including gold, with Minerven), (vi) [GL 52B](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/936831/download?inline) (transactions by U.S. entities with PdVSA), (vii) [GL 54B](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/936836/download?inline) (goods and services for mining operations), and (viii) [GL 61A](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/936841/download?inline) (telecommunications goods and services, including to CONATEL and CANTV). OFAC did not amend the contingent-contract licenses (GLs 49A, 55, 56, and 62) or the rest of the program, as they do not contain the deleted condition. The Annexes did not change either.

## The Change: The U.S.-Law Requirement Disappears

Until August 26, each license imposed two requirements on every contract with the Government of Venezuela, PdVSA, Minerven, or their subsidiaries. First, that "the terms of the contract are construed in accordance with the laws of a state or other jurisdiction within the United States." Second, that disputes be resolved "in the United States, the United Kingdom, France, or Singapore." As of August 27, only the second remains. With the first went the Note that "permitted" recognition of Venezuelan regulatory law in permits, concessions, and labor and environmental matters. If the license no longer imposes any law, there is no need to authorize exceptions to it.

It is worth measuring what OFAC removed. In June we wrote that the "construction" clause demanded less than the prior governing-law clause. OFAC read it strictly. [FAQ 1260](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/1260), published on that same June 10 and archived on August 27, specified that the law of a state of the United States had to govern the contractual matters between the parties: Performance, breach, remedies, payment, termination, validity, assignment, and enforceability. In practice, it remained a governing-law clause. The August amendment does not qualify it: It deletes it.

## What OFAC Said in Its FAQs

New [FAQ 1267](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/1267) answers "No" to the question whether the licenses require a choice-of-law clause in contracts with the Government of Venezuela. And it explains why: OFAC amended the licenses "in response to investment reforms undertaken by the Government of Venezuela since January 2026." It adds that the U.S. government "continues to support the efforts of U.S. companies to reinvest in Venezuela." It is, as noted, the first time OFAC has expressly tied a decision under its Venezuela program to the content of Venezuelan law.

[FAQ 1268](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/1268), also new, confirms that the forum clause remains mandatory. And it clarifies that it "refers to the jurisdiction in which dispute resolution proceedings must occur, not the law governing the contract." Amended [FAQ 1233](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/1233) reiterates that the clause need appear only in the direct contract with the Government of Venezuela or with the blocked person each license names. It does not apply to the insurance, charter, or service contracts that revolve around the transaction. And in amended [FAQ 1244](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/1244), governing law no longer appears among the restrictions OFAC expects to see in GL 49 contingent contracts. (One detail for the attentive reader: FAQs 1267, 1268, and 1233 say "GL 48B" where GL 48C is meant; all indications are that this is an OFAC typo.)

## Reading It Through Venezuelan Law

The reforms FAQ 1267 alludes to have names. The Law Amending the Organic Hydrocarbons Law (Official Gazette No. 6,978 Ext. of January 29, 2026) added a new Article 8. That article allows the parties to agree to settle disputes before Venezuelan courts or through alternative mechanisms, "including mediation and arbitration." The clauses must follow the guidelines to be set by the Ministry with competence in hydrocarbons in consultation with the Office of the Attorney General of the Republic, which have not been published to date; if they do follow them, they will not require the Attorney General's prior opinion. Its Article 26 guarantees the economic and financial balance of the operating companies' contracts against legal, tax, or regulatory changes. The Organic Mining Law (Official Gazette No. 7,020 Ext. of April 16, 2026) replicated both formulas in its Articles 9 and 71.

That framework explains why the U.S.-law requirement became an obstacle. Article 151 of the Constitution reads into public-interest contracts an implied clause: Disputes "shall be decided by the competent courts of the Republic, in accordance with its laws." The rule yields only where the nature of the contract makes it inapplicable. A contract with PdVSA or Minerven submitted wholesale to New York law, in our view, collided with that rule and forced the Venezuelan counterparty to negotiate against its own law. The August amendment does not, however, create an unrestricted freedom of choice: It removes a conflict. With the license requirement gone, the choice of law is once again governed by Venezuelan law, and there Article 29 of the Private International Law Act yields, in public-interest contracts, to Article 151 of the Constitution. What the parties recover is not the freedom to choose any law, but the freedom not to be pushed by the license toward one that their own legal system resists.

There lies the tension that the August round does not resolve. Article 8 of the Organic Hydrocarbons Law and Article 9 of the Organic Mining Law authorize the parties to submit their disputes to Venezuelan courts. The license does not allow it: It requires dispute resolution proceedings to occur in the United States, the United Kingdom, France, or Singapore. OFAC relaxed the governing law by invoking the Venezuelan reforms and, at the same time, preserved the one condition that those reforms contradict. If the parties choose the path the new Article 8 enables —the courts of the Republic— the U.S. counterparty falls outside the license.

## What Does It Mean in Practice?

**For those drafting contracts under these licenses:** Governing law is once again a commercial decision, not a license condition. A contract governed by Venezuelan law, English law, or the law of a state of the United States, with disputes in any of the four jurisdictions, complies with the license. In our view, contracts signed under the June standard, or under the March standard, continue to comply with the new text, which requires less, not more. It is worth checking whether the clause drafted "to comply with OFAC" is still the one that best serves the business, because the Venezuelan counterparty may ask to renegotiate it.

It is worth specifying what satisfies the forum clause when the chosen route is arbitration. An arbitration seated in New York, London, Paris, or Singapore complies, whatever institution administers it and whatever the substantive law. One seated in Caracas, Geneva, Madrid, or Mexico City does not. FAQ 1268 requires the proceedings to "occur" in one of the four jurisdictions, but does not clarify whether the requirement refers to the legal seat of the arbitration or to the physical venue of the hearings. Until OFAC clarifies this, the prudent course is for the two to coincide and for the clause to say so.

**For non-U.S. counterparties:** The oil companies listed in the GL 50C Annex and the traders operating under GLs 46D and 47B may accept Venezuelan law or agree on the law of their own jurisdiction. Neither option takes them outside the license. The limit remains the forum, and only in the direct contract with the Venezuelan counterparty.

**For the Venezuelan counterparty:** The State, PdVSA, Minerven, CONATEL, and CANTV may require Venezuelan law without pushing their counterparty outside the authorization. The negotiation shifts to the forum, where the hydrocarbons and mining laws already admit arbitration under the guidelines of the Ministry and the Attorney General. How to reconcile a foreign arbitral seat with Article 151 of the Constitution remains to be seen, contract by contract. The answer will depend on the nature of the contract and on the content of those guidelines.

## What Did Not Change

The rest of the scaffolding remains intact. Payments to blocked persons —other than local taxes, permits, and fees— are still channeled to the Foreign Government Deposit Funds under [Executive Order 14373](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/01/15/2026-00831/safeguarding-venezuelan-oil-revenue-for-the-good-of-the-american-and-venezuelan-people) in GLs 46D, 48C, 50C, 51C, 52B, and 54B. The exclusions for Russia, Iran, North Korea, Cuba, and China remain, license by license, on the same terms. Reporting is unchanged: The reports under the hydrocarbons, petrochemicals, and electricity licenses (GLs 46D, 47B, 48C, 50C, and 52B) go to the Department of State and the Department of Energy, while those under the minerals licenses (GLs 51C and 54B) go to the Department of State and the Department of the Interior. The first is due 10 days after the first transaction and the following ones every 90 days; only under GL 51C are they every 30 days and must include the due diligence documentation on the chain of custody of the minerals. The prohibition on forming new joint ventures in Venezuela under GLs 48C, 51C, 54B, and 61A remains in force. And OFAC retains the authority to revoke, amend, or restrict any of the eight licenses at any time.

## The Strategic Takeaway

Three signals emerge from the August round. First: OFAC calibrates its licenses to Venezuelan legislation. FAQ 1267 ties the amendment to the 2026 reforms, which turns the sanctions program into an instrument that responds to —and, in practice, rewards— changes in the legal framework for investment. Second: The forum is the safeguard Washington keeps. What matters to OFAC is where disputes with the Venezuelan State and its companies are decided, not under which law. Third: Speed. Amending GL 61 six days after issuing it shows that the contractual clause is by now a standard across the whole program. Any new sectoral license can be expected to be born with the same language.

None of this amounts to a lifting of sanctions. The general blocking of the Government of Venezuela under Executive Order 13884 remains in place. These licenses are windows within it, and each one keeps its exclusions, its reporting, and the financial control of the Foreign Government Deposit Funds. But the direction is consistent with what the March-to-June sequence already showed: A regime that is standardizing, taking in feedback from operators and, now, engaging with Venezuelan law.

For any company operating or considering operating under these licenses, our recommendation does not change: Obtain specialized legal advice before closing the next contract. The freedom to choose the governing law opens up options, but it shifts to the parties a decision that used to be imposed. And responsibility for complying with the remaining conditions still rests entirely on whoever uses the license.
