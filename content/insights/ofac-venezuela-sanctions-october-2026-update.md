> **Update, November 4, 2026.** After this article was written, OFAC issued [GL 5AA](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/937321/download), which authorizes certain transactions related to the PdVSA 2020 8.5 percent bond only on or after December 18, 2026, pushing back the November 5 date. The references below to November 5 and to GL 5Z describe the situation before GL 5AA.

## At a Glance

- **September 28.** GLs 46E, 48D and 49B: methanol enters the Annex of petrochemical products. The body of the licenses does not change.
- **September 30 and October 13.** Five Federal Register notices codify licenses already issued. They create no new authorizations.
- **October 5 and 14.** OFAC removes Tibisay Lucena Ramírez, Ramón Darío Vivas Velasco, Jorge Luis García Carneiro and Jorge Jesús Rodríguez Gómez from the SDN List.
- **October 16.** GL 60A: the earthquake relief authorization is extended to April 21, 2027, without the FGDF requirement.
- **November 5.** Deferred effective date under GL 5Z. As of this writing, there is no later version.

![Infographic: OFAC Venezuela — what changed in October and what expires in November](/images/insights/ofac-venezuela-october-2026-update.html)

Since September 30, 2026, the Office of Foreign Assets Control ("OFAC") of the U.S. Department of the Treasury has not issued any general license ("GL") with a new number for Venezuela. Even so, it moved pieces that matter: it added methanol to the annex of three sectoral licenses, extended the earthquake relief license one week before it expired and removed four individuals from the [Specially Designated Nationals and Blocked Persons List](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/topic/1631) ("SDN List"). It also published, in the Federal Register, versions of licenses that were already public.

There is also an upcoming date. On November 5, the deferral under GL 5Z, the license for the 2020 bond of Petróleos de Venezuela ("PdVSA") and the CITGO collateral, will expire. This article lays out what happened.

## How We Got Here

Four recent milestones frame this month:

- **August 27.** OFAC amended eight licenses (GLs 46D, 47B, 48C, 50C, 51C, 52B, 54B and 61A), removed the U.S.-law requirement and kept the forum clause (see José P. Barnola Jr. and Raúl Sancristóbal, [OFAC Removes the U.S.-Law Requirement from Eight Venezuela General Licenses](/insights/ofac-removes-us-law-requirement-venezuela-general-licenses-august-2026)).
- **September 2.** GLs 51D, 54C and 55A brought coal into the mining licenses (see José P. Barnola Jr. and Raúl Sancristóbal, [OFAC Extends the Scope of Its Mining Licenses to Include Coal](/insights/ofac-extends-mining-licenses-coal-venezuela-september-2026)).
- **September 14.** GL 52C allowed blocked PdVSA officers to sign contracts in their official capacity and fenced off CITGO's governance (see José P. Barnola Jr. and Raúl Sancristóbal, [OFAC Lets Blocked PdVSA Officers Sign, and Fences Off CITGO](/insights/ofac-pdvsa-officers-sign-citgo-governance-september-2026)).
- **September 16.** GL 5Z deferred to November 5 the effective date of the authorization for the PdVSA 2020 bond.

## Methanol Enters the Annex: GLs 46E, 48D and 49B

On September 28, 2026, OFAC replaced GL 46D with [GL 46E](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/936996/download), GL 48C with [GL 48D](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/937001/download) and GL 49A with [GL 49B](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/937006/download). The body of the three licenses is identical to that of their previous versions. The only substantive change is in the Annex of "petrochemical products" that the three share: OFAC added methanol (methyl alcohol) with five Harmonized System codes (2905111000, 2905112010, 2905112015, 2905112085 and 9817290200).

The change matters because each license defines "petrochemical products" to include fertilizers and their chemical precursors, including those in the Annex. With methanol on the list, the effect is as follows:

- **GL 46E.** An established U.S. entity (organized in the United States or in one of its jurisdictions on or before January 29, 2025) may buy, store, transport and sell Venezuelan-origin methanol for import into the United States, including in transactions with the Government of Venezuela, PdVSA or entities in which PdVSA holds 50 percent or more.
- **GL 48D.** A U.S. person, or anyone acting from the United States, may supply goods, technology, software and services for the production of petrochemical products in Venezuela, now with methanol expressly included.
- **GL 49B.** Parties may negotiate and sign contingent contracts for new investment in the petrochemical sector, provided that performance is expressly contingent on separate OFAC authorization.

The conditions did not change. Under GLs 46E and 48D, contracts with the Government of Venezuela, PdVSA or its entities must require that dispute resolution proceedings take place in the United States, the United Kingdom, France or Singapore. Any monetary payment to a blocked person, other than local taxes, permits and fees, must be made into the Foreign Government Deposit Funds ("FGDF") under [Executive Order 14373](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/01/15/2026-00831/safeguarding-venezuelan-oil-revenue-for-the-good-of-the-american-and-venezuelan-people). The exclusions for any nexus with Russia, Iran, North Korea and Cuba remain, as does the China exclusion on the terms of each license. The reporting requirement in paragraph (c) of GL 46E reaches only those who export oil to countries other than the United States; it does not reach those who trade methanol.

For exporters, traders and suppliers in the sector, the inclusion removes a classification debate. The remaining work is contractual, and it covers the forum clause, the FGDF payment mechanism and confirmation that the U.S. counterparty meets the established-entity requirement.

OFAC's Frequently Asked Questions ("FAQ") [1233](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/1233), [1267](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/1267) and [1268](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/1268) still cite earlier versions of the licenses (GLs 46D, 48B, 51C, 52B and 54B, among others), and [FAQ 1247](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/1247) cites GLs 46D, 51D and 52B. OFAC has not updated them to reflect the new versions. We understand that their content applies to the current versions, but that correspondence is our reading, not a confirmation from OFAC.

## The Federal Register (the Official Journal of the U.S. Federal Government): What It Codifies and What It Does Not

On September 30, OFAC published four notices in the Federal Register (91 FR 61764, 61771, which carries two notices, and 61775) containing licenses it had already issued on its website: GLs 46D, 47B, 48C, 50C, 51C, 52B, 54B and 61A; GL 5W; GLs 52 through 58; and GLs 46C, 47A, 48B, 50B, 51B, 52A and 54A. On October 13, it did the same with GL 60 (91 FR 64758).

These publications codify and do not authorize. They create no new permissions and alter no conditions. Their value is archival and evidentiary, and their risk is confusion, because most of the published versions have already been replaced. Anyone who cites GL 46D because they read it in the Federal Register cites a superseded license. According to OFAC's index, the sectoral-package licenses in force today include GLs 46E, 47B, 48D, 49B, 50C, 51D, 52C, 54C, 55A and 61A.

## GL 60A: Six More Months for Earthquake Relief

GL 60, dated June 25, 2026, authorized all transactions related to earthquake relief in Venezuela that the Venezuela Sanctions Regulations (31 CFR part 591, "VSR") would otherwise prohibit. It was set to expire at 12:01 a.m. eastern daylight time on October 23, 2026. On October 16, one week earlier, OFAC replaced it with [GL 60A](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/937251/download), which extends the authorization to 12:01 a.m. EDT on April 21, 2027.

The operative text is identical. Only the date changes, and a new paragraph (c) supersedes GL 60. Note 1 remains, and it allows funds to be processed to or from Venezuela on behalf of third-country persons. It also allows financial institutions to rely on the originator of a transfer, unless they know or have reason to know that it does not comply with the license. The limits also remain: the license does not unblock property or authorize transactions prohibited by other executive orders or by other parts of 31 CFR chapter V.

[FAQ 1263](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/1263), updated on October 16, confirms that payments to the Government of Venezuela for taxes, tolls and fees connected with the relief do not go into the FGDF. But it flags what matters most in practice: payments for activities outside GL 60A that are authorized under licenses with an FGDF requirement must still go into the FGDF and may not be recharacterized as earthquake relief. The link to the relief must be real and documented.

FAQ 1263 still cites GL 48C, which GL 48D superseded on September 28, and lists GL 49B among the licenses with an FGDF requirement. The text of GL 49B contains no such requirement: it only authorizes negotiating and signing contingent contracts. In our reading, the text of the license prevails. Even so, until OFAC corrects the FAQ, we recommend recording that analysis in the compliance file. According to [FAQ 1244](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/1244), the specific license that authorizes performance of a contingent contract could impose the FGDF requirement.

## Removals from the SDN List: What They Mean and What They Do Not

On October 5, OFAC announced the third round of its sanctions modernization initiative: it removed 125 individuals and entities from its lists and updated 22 entries. The removals include three individuals from the Venezuela program, namely Tibisay Lucena Ramírez (who died in 2023), Ramón Darío Vivas Velasco (who died in 2020) and Jorge Luis García Carneiro (who died in 2021) (the latter with two alias entries). On October 14, OFAC also removed Jorge Jesús Rodríguez Gómez, with five alias entries. The October 5 announcement states that those removed include deceased individuals, defunct companies and entries with insufficient information for screening, but it does not give the reason for each removal.

For those who screen counterparties, the reading has three levels:

- **What changes.** Removal from the SDN List lifts the blocking that applied to the person because of the designation. A transaction that previously required rejection or blocking can now be analyzed under ordinary criteria. Removal does not change other jurisdictions' lists or the internal risk policies of banks and counterparties.
- **What does not change.** Executive Order 13884 blocks the Government of Venezuela, and its definition (section 6(d)) includes those who act or purport to act on its behalf. Anyone who keeps acting on behalf of the Government of Venezuela remains within that definition, even if they no longer appear on the SDN List by name.
- **What to do.** Determine, case by case, whether the person acts on behalf of the Government of Venezuela and which license covers the transaction, and document the analysis. GL 57, for example, covers financial services involving state-owned banks and certain Government of Venezuela individuals blocked solely under Executive Order 13884.

Removing a name from the SDN List does not amount to an OFAC determination of the person's status under the definition of Government of Venezuela.

## What Comes Next: GL 5Z and November 5

GL 5Z, dated September 16, defers to November 5, 2026 the effectiveness of the authorization for transactions related to the PdVSA 2020 8.5 percent bond, which is secured by CITGO Holding shares. Since GL 5A (October 24, 2019), OFAC has extended that deferral in short blocks and usually publishes the next version one or two days before the deadline. As of October 30, OFAC had not published a version later than GL 5Z.

Until the authorization takes effect, any sale or transfer of CITGO shares connected with the bond remains prohibited without a specific license ([FAQ 595](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/595)). If OFAC does not issue a new version before November 5, the GL 5Z authorization will take effect and change that picture. Based on the historical pattern, that is not the most likely scenario; but it cannot be ruled out either, and anyone with exposure to CITGO should have a plan for both outcomes. In addition, GL 52C prohibits any transaction that affects the governance of PDV Holding, CITGO Holding and CITGO Petroleum.

## The Strategic Takeaway

October was a month of maintenance for the regulatory framework. OFAC adjusted an annex, extended a humanitarian license without touching its text and cleaned up its list. The conditions that structure the package remain intact (a forum in four jurisdictions, payments into the FGDF, an established U.S. entity and the exclusion of certain countries). What changes is the detail.
