## At a Glance

- **What changed.** As of September 14, 2026, GL 52C allows PdVSA officers on the SDN List to sign the contracts the license authorizes. And it excludes any transaction to affect or alter the governance of PDV Holding, CITGO Holding and CITGO Petroleum.
- **What remains.** The dispute resolution forum in the United States, the United Kingdom, France or Singapore, the channeling of payments into the Foreign Government Deposit Funds, and the remaining exclusions of GL 52B.
- **Why it matters.** A designated person's signature is no longer an obstacle to closing, and the CITGO chain sits outside what this general license can reach.
- **What to review.** The corporate evidence of the PdVSA signer before each closing, and FAQ 1247, which is still written around GL 52B.

![Infographic: OFAC lets blocked PdVSA officers sign, and fences off CITGO — GL 52C (September 14, 2026)](/images/insights/ofac-gl52c-pdvsa-citgo-september-2026.html)

On September 14, 2026, the Office of Foreign Assets Control ("OFAC") of the U.S. Department of the Treasury [amended a single license](https://ofac.treasury.gov/recent-actions/20260914) under the Venezuela program. [GL 52C](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/936926/download) replaces and supersedes GL 52B in its entirety under the [Venezuela Sanctions Regulations](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-V/part-591) (31 CFR part 591). It keeps what that license authorized: transactions involving Petróleos de Venezuela, S.A. ("PdVSA") and its subsidiaries prohibited by Executive Orders 13884 and 13850. Who may use it remains the established U.S. entity, meaning one organized under the laws of the United States, or of any jurisdiction within it, on or before January 29, 2025.

The amendment does two things, and they point in opposite directions. It allows PdVSA officers listed as specially designated nationals (SDN) to sign the contracts the license authorizes. And it bars anyone from using that same license to alter the governance of PDV Holding, CITGO Holding and CITGO Petroleum. It does not expand sectors or add authorized entities.

The action continues the sequence OFAC has deployed throughout 2026, which we have analyzed in previous installments:

- **March.** Four licenses defined the sectoral scope of the new framework —hydrocarbons, petrochemicals, electricity and minerals— (see José P. Barnola Jr., [Four New OFAC Licenses: The New Framework for Doing Business with Venezuela](/insights/ofac-general-licenses-venezuela-march-2026)).
- **April.** Two licenses enabled contingent contracts and payment channels (see José P. Barnola Jr. and Raúl Sancristóbal, [Two New OFAC Licenses: Contingent Contracts and Financial Services with Venezuela](/insights/ofac-general-licenses-venezuela-april-2026)).
- **May.** One license opened the advisory layer for a potential debt restructuring (see José P. Barnola Jr. and Raúl Sancristóbal, [New OFAC License: Advisory Services for a Potential Restructuring of Venezuelan Debt](/insights/ofac-general-license-58-venezuela-debt-restructuring-advisory-services)).
- **June 10.** Seven energy and mining licenses standardized their contractual clauses (see José P. Barnola Jr. and Raúl Sancristóbal, [OFAC Amends Seven Venezuela General Licenses and Standardizes Their Contractual Framework](/insights/ofac-amended-seven-venezuela-general-licenses-june-2026)).
- **August 27.** OFAC [removed the U.S.-law requirement](https://ofac.treasury.gov/recent-actions/20260827) from eight licenses (see José P. Barnola Jr. and Raúl Sancristóbal, [OFAC Removes the U.S.-Law Requirement from Eight Venezuela General Licenses](/insights/ofac-removes-us-law-requirement-venezuela-general-licenses-august-2026)).
- **September 2.** [GLs 51D, 54C and 55A](https://ofac.treasury.gov/recent-actions/20260902) extended the minerals perimeter to coal (see José P. Barnola Jr. and Raúl Sancristóbal, [OFAC Extends the Scope of Its Mining Licenses to Include Coal](/insights/ofac-extends-mining-licenses-coal-venezuela-september-2026)).
- **September 14.** GL 52C returns to PdVSA's core license, 12 days later, and moves from contract clauses to people and structures.

## The Text of GL 52C

The text of GL 52C is the text of GL 52B plus two new paragraphs and the renumbering that accommodates them. The former exclusions paragraph (c) became paragraph (d), and the ones that followed each moved down a letter. Everything else survives without substantive change.

The two conditions the license imposes did not move either. Contracts with PdVSA must submit disputes to the United States, the United Kingdom, France or Singapore. And monetary payments to blocked persons — other than local taxes, permits and fees — must be channeled into the Foreign Government Deposit Funds created by [Executive Order 14373](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/01/15/2026-00831/safeguarding-venezuelan-oil-revenue-for-the-good-of-the-american-and-venezuelan-people), or into any other account Treasury instructs.

## Who May Sign for PdVSA

The first change solves an operational problem the license had been carrying. GL 52B authorized contracting with PdVSA, but its exclusion of designated persons reached any individual on the SDN List, including PdVSA's own directors and attorneys-in-fact.

New paragraph (c) authorizes transactions ordinarily incident and necessary for blocked individuals to execute and sign contracts, agreements and other transaction documents. The exclusion of designated persons, now in paragraph (d)(3), opens with the phrase "except as authorized by paragraph (c)."

The authorization covers execution and signature, not general dealings with that person. The individual must act solely in an official capacity as an officer, employee or authorized representative of PdVSA or one of its subsidiaries. Anyone signing for another entity, or in a personal capacity, falls outside.

The coverage reaches only paragraph (a) transactions, not those under paragraph (b) or under other licenses in the program. And none of this unblocks property. The person remains on the SDN List for every other purpose, and assets within U.S. jurisdiction remain frozen.

## The Governance of the CITGO Chain Falls Outside

The second change runs the other way. New paragraph (d)(9) excludes from the authorization any transaction to affect or alter the governance of PDV Holding, Inc., Citgo Holding, Inc. or CITGO Petroleum Corporation. The exclusion names the appointment, removal and replacement of any director, officer or other corporate governance official. OFAC wrote "the governance" without qualifying it, so the exclusion reaches further than formal board action.

GL 52B already closed the door to altering CITGO's ownership. By reference to Executive Order 13835, it did not authorize the Government of Venezuela to sell, transfer, assign or pledge equity interests in PdVSA or its subsidiaries. That category includes the PDV Holding and CITGO chain. GL 52C closes control separately.

## What OFAC Said in Its FAQs

[FAQ 1245](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/1245), which describes the scope of the license, was amended on that same September 14 and reflects both changes. Among what GL 52C does not authorize it lists two separate items. One, transactions transferring equity interest in PDV Holding, CITGO Holding or CITGO Petroleum. The other, transactions to affect or alter the governance of those three. The FAQ puts both doors the license closes in plain view, ownership and control.

[FAQ 1247](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/1247), which describes the safe harbor for non-U.S. persons operating under these licenses, is still written around GL 52B. OFAC amended it on September 2 and, as this goes to press, has not touched it since, so it still cites a license that no longer exists. We estimate this is a lag in maintenance rather than a change in position, but we recommend against relying on it without flagging the point.

Both are non-binding interpretive guidance.

## The View from Venezuelan Law

The need for new paragraph (c) is easier to understand from Venezuelan law than from U.S. law. Who may bind a stock corporation is not for its counterparty to decide. Article 213, paragraph 8, of the Commercial Code requires the charter and bylaws to state which members of the managing board may sign for the company. And Article 243 warns that directors may not undertake transactions other than those expressly set out in the bylaws, on pain of personal liability toward third parties and toward the company.

A U.S. company could not ask PdVSA to send someone other than the person its bylaws authorize. In our view, a signature from an unauthorized person exposes the contract to challenge and the signer to personal liability. A power of attorney granted to a third party did not help either, unless granted by whoever does represent the company, because the power carries the same limitation. U.S. sanctions on individuals collided with the Venezuelan rule on corporate representation, and the result was a license authorizing transactions that, in practice, no one could safely sign.

## What Does It Mean in Practice?

**For those drafting contracts under this license:** a designated person's signature is no longer an obstacle, and a verification burden appears. Three things are worth documenting before signing. First, that the signer holds the office invoked, evidenced by the board minutes or the power of attorney. Second, that PdVSA's bylaws, or those of the subsidiary, grant that person signing authority. Third, that the contract records that the person acts in an official and not a personal capacity.

We consider it prudent to keep that support in the compliance file, because the authorization rests on a fact — the signer's official capacity — that whoever uses the license must be able to prove later.

**For non-U.S. counterparties:** the FAQ 1247 safe harbor remains available on its terms, but the FAQ names GL 52B. Until OFAC updates it, compliance with its conditions is worth documenting by reference to the operative text of GL 52C.

**For the Venezuelan counterparty:** PdVSA may sign through the corporate bodies its bylaws authorize, without pushing its counterparty outside the authorization.

**For anyone with an interest in the PDV Holding and CITGO structure:** paragraph (d)(9) is a closed door, not an omission. We understand that any transaction touching boards, appointments or removals in that chain requires a specific OFAC license, and that whether it is granted will turn on considerations beyond the scope of this general license.

## What Did Not Change

The rest of the scaffolding remains intact. The license does not authorize transactions in PdVSA bonds and debt prohibited by Executive Order 13808, including agreements to settle them. Nor does it authorize settlement agreements or the enforcement of liens, judgments, arbitral awards or other orders against blocked property. The exclusions for payment terms that are not commercially reasonable, debt swaps, payments in gold and Government of Venezuela digital currency remain.

So do the exclusions for Russia, Iran, North Korea and Cuba, and for Venezuelan or U.S. entities owned or controlled by, or in a joint venture with, persons from China. Unblocking property and dealings with blocked vessels remain outside.

The reporting did not move either. Anyone exporting, selling or supplying Venezuelan-origin oil or petrochemical products to countries other than the United States must report to the Departments of State and Energy. The first report is due 10 days after the first transaction and the following ones every 90 days.

The closing Note to the license still warns that nothing in it relieves any person from compliance with the requirements of other federal agencies, including the Department of Commerce's Bureau of Industry and Security.

And, as with every general license in the program, OFAC keeps the power to revoke, modify or restrict it at any time.

## The Strategic Takeaway

Three signals emerge from this amendment. The first is that OFAC is tuning its licenses to practice rather than to policy. The signature problem was not raised by a sanctions debate but by a transactional lawyer who could not close. That OFAC addressed it with a single paragraph suggests the feedback channel with operators works, and that it is worth using.

The second is that the opening has a geographic limit. OFAC is widening what can be done in Venezuela while tightening what can be done with Venezuelan assets in the United States. CITGO is not a chip in the commercial reopening, and the amendment says so by name.

The third is structural. Paragraph (d)(9) closes the control route to the PDV Holding and CITGO chain. The ownership route was already closed as to transfers by the Government of Venezuela, and on September 16 OFAC closed the one that remained.

[GL 5Z](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/936946/download) deferred to November 5, 2026 the authorization for holders of the PdVSA 2020 8.5 percent bond to reach their collateral, which is CITGO Holding stock. OFAC signed it one day before the previous deadline expired.

In 48 hours OFAC reinforced both routes to the same structure. [FAQ 595](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/595) keeps its favorable licensing policy toward an agreement to restructure the bond, so the CITGO chain is not sealed off but routed to specific licensing.

None of this amounts to a lifting of sanctions. The blanket block of the Government of Venezuela under Executive Order 13884 remains in force. This license is a window within it, and it keeps its exclusions, its reporting and the financial control of the Foreign Government Deposit Funds.

For any company operating or evaluating operations under it, the advice is the usual one. Obtain specialized legal counsel before signing, and in this case before deciding who signs.
