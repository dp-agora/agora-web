*Decree No. 5,460 repeals the resolutions that made exports of fishery, aquaculture and coffee products subject to prior authorization, eliminates Legal Regimes 4, 14 and 18 of the Customs Tariff and the SUNAGRO codes and transit permits as conditions for exporting, and requires the State to continue issuing the certificates demanded by the destination market. It will enter into force on November 19, 2026.*

For almost a decade, exporting fish, shrimp or coffee from Venezuela required more than a buyer abroad. It took the approval of a ministerial commission, a permit listed in the Customs Tariff, a code from the National Superintendency of Agri-Food Management ("SUNAGRO") and a transit permit (*guía de movilización*). Resolution DM/No. 0021-17 of the Ministry of People's Power for Fisheries and Aquaculture (Official Gazette ("O.G.") No. 41,168 of June 8, 2017) ("Resolution 21") created an Evaluation Commission with power to approve or deny each export application and to set exportable quantities *"subject to prior satisfaction of domestic demand."* Joint Resolution DM No. 080/2018 and DM No. 032/2018 of the Ministries of People's Power for Productive Agriculture and Land and for Food (O.G. No. 41,517 of November 5, 2018) ("2018 Joint Resolution") subjected the entire coffee chain to registration with the Venezuelan Coffee Corporation and SUNAGRO, and justified controls on the movement of coffee beans by the need to verify that they were not intended for export.

Decree No. 5,460 (O.G. No. 7,091 Ext. of November 4, 2026) ("Decree") reverses that logic. Its premise is set out in the second recital: to remove the requirements that domestic law imposes as a condition for exporting, without affecting the issuance of the certificates and documents required by destination countries. The State no longer decides whether goods may be exported; it now provides a service to the exporter. The Decree is based on article 11(1) of the Law on the Promotion of Non-Oil Exports (O.G. No. 6,824 Ext. of July 18, 2024) ("Promotion Law"), which empowers the National Executive to remove any procedures that are not essential for exporting.

## What is repealed: three sector-specific resolutions

Article 2 repeals the resolutions that *"regulate, reserve, control, prohibit, authorize or restrict"* exports of fishery and aquaculture products and by-products, coffee and cocoa. The list is closed:

1. **Resolution 21 (fisheries and aquaculture).** The Evaluation Commission for Imports and Exports of Fishery and Aquaculture Products and By-products disappears, together with its Executive Committee, its Technical Committee and its 10-calendar-day term to decide each application. With it goes the export quota tied to prior satisfaction of domestic demand. The repeal is complete, so it also reaches the review of imports that the Commission was in charge of.
2. **2018 Joint Resolution (coffee).** It required producers, processing plants, roasters and related companies to register in the Integrated Agri-Food Control System ("SICA"), to hold a minimum set of equipment, and to obtain a single SUNAGRO transit permit, subject to prior transport authorization from the Venezuelan Coffee Corporation.
3. **Resolution DM/No. 017/2023 of the Ministry of People's Power for Productive Agriculture and Land (O.G. No. 42,721 of September 25, 2023) ("Resolution 17").** It regulated coffee producer associations or processing plants and roasters: mandatory receipt of coffee from all producers, a minimum price for green coffee not lower than the international price or the national cost structure, sales to the domestic roasting industry with no limitation other than payment of the price, and, to move green coffee, a SUNAGRO transit permit, a phytosanitary certificate from the National Institute of Integral Agricultural Health, and an authorization from the Venezuelan Coffee Corporation. On exports, it only provided for advisory support to coffee growers.

Article 2 announces the repeal of rules on coffee and cocoa, but none of the three resolutions listed deals with cocoa. Because the list is exhaustive (*"namely"*), any other rule that conditions cocoa exports is not repealed by this route. For cocoa exporters, relief comes from article 3 and, going forward, from the review of the Tariff that article 7 entrusts to COMEX. In addition, the repeal of the two coffee resolutions is complete: it also reaches their rules on registration, equipment and domestic transport, without prejudice to obligations arising from other SUNAGRO regulations. The full repeal of Resolution 17 also eliminates rules unrelated to exports, such as the minimum price for green coffee and the obligation of processing plants to sell to domestic roasters. The coffee sector should bear that effect in mind.

## What is no longer required to export

Article 3 eliminates three groups of requirements *"as essential requirements for export"*:

- **Certificate of Good Manufacturing Practices for Export issued by the Autonomous Sanitary Comptroller Service ("SACS").** It is no longer a domestic condition for exporting. SACS must continue to issue it when the destination country requires it.
- **Legal Regimes 4, 14 and 18.** These are the permits of the ministries with competence over industry and national production (4), food (14), and fisheries and aquaculture (18), coded in article 21 of the Customs Tariff enacted by Decree No. 4,944 (O.G. No. 6,804 Ext. of April 25, 2024), as amended ("Tariff"). Legal Regime 14 appears in the export column for wheat, corn, rice, corn and rice flour, soybean oil, sugar, *panela* (unrefined whole cane sugar) and animal feed. Legal Regime 4 appears in the export column for iron ore and certain chemical products.
- **SUNAGRO export codes and transit permits.** The elimination covers codes and transit permits *"for export."* SUNAGRO controls over domestic transport that have a different legal basis are not affected.

Article 3 does not distinguish between goods. In the export column of the Tariff, Legal Regime 4 does not only apply to food: it also applies to chemical precursors and ozone-depleting substances. Those products remain subject to their special laws, such as the Organic Drug Law, and to other Legal Regimes of the Tariff, such as Legal Regime 10 (ecosocialism), which the Decree does not affect. Before exporting them, exporters should confirm which other Legal Regimes remain in column 6 of the Tariff for the relevant tariff subheading.

## What the State must continue to issue

The Decree does not dismantle official certification. It redirects it: the document is no longer a permit to leave the country, but a service to enter the destination market.

- **SACS (art. 4).** It must issue Certificates of Good Manufacturing Practices for Export, Certificates of Free Sale for Export and any other certification within its competence, when the destination country requires them, under the Organic Health Law and Resolution No. SG-457-96 of the former Ministry of Health and Social Assistance (Standards on Good Practices for the Manufacture, Storage and Transport of Food for Human Consumption) (O.G. No. 36,081 of November 7, 1996).
- **Socialist Institute of Fisheries and Aquaculture (art. 5).** It must issue Sanitary Certificates for Export and Certificates of Legal Catch or Responsible Fishing for Export, and keep the registers of establishments and vessels and the official controls required by destination countries.
- **All public bodies and entities (art. 6).** They will continue to issue, at the exporter's request, sanitary, phytosanitary, zoosanitary, food safety and origin certificates, and any other document needed for the goods to enter the destination country or to be placed in international markets, *"without the need to prove that they are required by the destination country."*

There is a tension between articles 4 and 5 of the Decree, which make issuance conditional on the document being required by the destination country, and article 6, which releases the exporter from proving it. In our opinion, article 6 sets the procedural rule: the exporter applies and the agency issues, without requiring proof of the foreign regulation. Exporters should invoke it expressly in each application. There is also a commercial reason: many buyers require certificates that the law of the destination country does not impose, and article 6 covers them by referring to the placement of goods in international markets.

## Pending tasks

The Decree leaves three tasks open. Much of its actual reach depends on them:

1. **COMEX and the Tariff (art. 7).** The Presidential Commission known as the Foreign Trade Committee ("COMEX"), created by Decree No. 5,105 (O.G. No. 6,890 Ext. of March 6, 2025), may eliminate or waive, in whole or in part and on a general or case-by-case basis, the permits, certificates, registrations or records required to export under the Tariff. It must review the Tariff to eliminate any export restriction that is not based on health, chemical substance control, security or defense grounds. Requirements of the destination country and those arising from treaties are preserved. The Decree sets no deadline for that review.
2. **Sector regulations within 90 days (art. 8).** The ministries with competence over productive agriculture and land and over fisheries and aquaculture must issue sector development policies, *"excluding regulations on domestic trade, imports or exports."* The Decree does not indicate when that term starts. If counted from its entry into force, it will expire on February 17, 2027. The exclusion matters: the new rules should not reintroduce, through sector regulation, the controls the Decree removes.
3. **Legalization and apostille within 10 business days (art. 9).** The ministry with competence over foreign affairs and international trade must issue, by resolution, a simplified procedure to legalize and apostille the documents required in destination countries, with a maximum term of 10 business days for issuance. There is no deadline to issue that resolution either.

## SENIAT and customs

Article 10 includes the National Integrated Customs and Tax Administration Service ("SENIAT") among the bodies in charge of implementing the Decree. It is the most important practical piece. Legal Regimes are verified in the export customs declaration, and as long as the automated customs system keeps requiring Legal Regimes 4, 14 and 18, relief will exist in the Official Gazette but not at the port.

The Decree eliminates the enforceability of those Legal Regimes without amending article 37 of the Tariff, which still lists them in its export column. Both instruments are presidential decrees, and the Decree, as the later and more specific rule, prevails. We recommend carrying a copy of the Decree to every customs procedure and keeping a written record of any demand for the eliminated requirements from November 19, 2026 onwards.

## VAT refunds: what changes for exporters

The Decree does not amend the Value Added Tax Law (O.G. No. 6,507 Ext. of January 29, 2020) ("VAT Law"), but it does have an impact on it. More exporters and more shipments mean more input VAT to recover, and the refund regime largely determines whether exporting is profitable.

- **The starting point.** Exports of goods are taxed at a 0% rate (VAT Law, art. 27). Exporters generate no output VAT on their sales abroad, but they bear VAT on their purchases and imports (input VAT). Article 43 of the VAT Law grants them the right to recover the input VAT borne in connection with their export activity.
- **Exporters of exempt goods.** Several products covered by the Decree are VAT-exempt in the domestic market: roasted, ground or whole-bean coffee, rice, vegetable flours, sugar and edible oils, among others (VAT Law, art. 18). Exporters of those goods may also obtain refunds, provided they are registered in the special registry provided for in article 43 of the VAT Law.
- **The procedure.** One application per month is allowed, and it must cover a single tax period (which is monthly). SENIAT has 30 business days to decide, counted from the final receipt of the application. The refund is paid through special tax refund certificates, which may be assigned or used to pay national taxes. If SENIAT does not respond in time, the exporter may either wait or treat the application as denied and file a tax appeal before the tax courts (*recurso contencioso tributario*) (VAT Law, art. 43). Taxpayers that also sell in the domestic market may only recover the portion of input VAT attributable to their exports, according to the formula in the Sole Paragraph of article 43 of the VAT Law.
- **What SENIAT verifies.** That the exports actually took place; that they correspond to the period claimed; the domestic sales of the period; the purchases and imports that generated the input VAT; that domestic suppliers are ordinary VAT taxpayers; and that they have recorded the corresponding output VAT (VAT Law, art. 44).
- **Which documents are required.** Partial Regulation No. 1 of the VAT Law on the recovery of input VAT by exporters (O.G. No. 37,794 of October 10, 2003) requires a list of exports showing the serial number of the export declaration, the customs office of exit, the FOB value, the export invoice, the customs broker and the date of the shipping document, among other data (art. 8). Export permits are not on that list.

This last point has consequences. The Political-Administrative Chamber has ruled that SENIAT may not make a refund conditional on documents the regulations do not require. On that basis, it annulled the rejection of exports grounded on the lack of customs certification of the export declarations and held that verifying the suppliers' taxpayer status is the duty of the Tax Administration, not the exporter (see Supreme Tribunal of Justice, Political-Administrative Chamber, *Ferroatlántica de Venezuela Ferroven, S.A. v. National Treasury*, Decision of November 29, 2017). The Decree strengthens that reasoning: if Legal Regimes 4, 14 and 18 and the SUNAGRO codes are no longer required to export, even less can they be required to recover VAT. The customs declaration and the shipping document (bill of lading, air waybill or consignment note) remain the proof of export.

The Promotion Law instructs the National Executive to establish an expedited tax refund procedure for non-oil exports (*drawback*) and, exceptionally, for other national taxes (art. 21). The Decree does not develop that procedure. For VAT, the route remains articles 43 and 44 of the VAT Law and its Regulation. Three recommendations for the refund file:

- **Match the period.** For exports of goods, VAT accrues upon the final exit of the goods from the customs territory (VAT Law, art. 13(4)). The export must be recorded in the sales ledger and in the VAT return for the period in which it actually occurred. In *Ferroatlántica*, the exporter lost precisely the part of its claim in which it failed to prove that timing.
- **Document suppliers.** Recoverable input VAT depends on the supplier being an ordinary VAT taxpayer and having reported the output VAT (VAT Law, art. 44(5) and (6)). Case law places that verification on SENIAT, but a file with each supplier's tax ID (RIF), invoices and proof of payment shortens the discussion.
- **Register in the special registry** before the first application when exporting exempt goods.

## Before and after the Decree

| Matter | Until November 18, 2026 | From November 19, 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| **Fisheries and aquaculture** | Approval of each export by the Evaluation Commission (Resolution 21), with quotas subject to prior satisfaction of domestic demand. | Resolution 21 repealed (art. 2(1)). No prior authorization or quotas. |
| **Coffee** | Registration in SICA with the Venezuelan Coffee Corporation and SUNAGRO, transport authorization and SUNAGRO transit permit (2018 and 2023 Resolutions). | Both resolutions repealed (art. 2(2) and (3)). |
| **Legal Regimes 4, 14 and 18** | Required to export under column 6 of the Tariff. | Not required to export (art. 3(2)). Still apply to imports where the Tariff provides for them. |
| **SUNAGRO codes and transit permits** | Required to export. | Not required to export (art. 3(3)). |
| **Good Manufacturing Practices (SACS)** | Condition for exporting. | Only if required by the destination country; SACS must issue it (arts. 3(1) and 4). |
| **Sanitary, phytosanitary, origin and other certificates** | Issued by the competent bodies. | Issued at the exporter's request, without proof of the destination country's requirement (art. 6). |
| **Other Tariff permits** | Required. | Still required. COMEX may eliminate or waive them, except on health, chemical substance, security or defense grounds (art. 7). |
| **VAT refunds** | Arts. 43 and 44 of the VAT Law and its Regulation. | No regulatory changes. The eliminated requirements cannot be demanded as support for the application. |

## What did not change

- **VUCE registration.** The Promotion Law requires every non-oil exporter to register with the Single Window for Foreign Trade (VUCE) and obtain the corresponding certificate (art. 14). It is a statutory requirement that a decree cannot remove.
- **Destination-country and treaty requirements.** They are expressly preserved (arts. 1 and 7).
- **The other Legal Regimes of the Tariff.** Legal Regimes other than 4, 14 and 18 remain enforceable until COMEX eliminates or waives them.
- **Conditions for Promotion Law incentives.** To access them, exporters must comply with Central Bank of Venezuela rules on the mandatory sale of a percentage of the foreign currency earned from exports (art. 22).
- **The import regime.** Except for the full repeal of Resolution 21, which also covered imports, the Decree deals only with exports.

## What to do before and after November 19, 2026

- **Review the tariff classification** of each product and confirm which Legal Regimes remain in the export column of the Tariff besides those eliminated.
- **Do not apply the Decree early.** Until November 18, 2026, the repealed resolutions and requirements remain in force. Shipments scheduled before that date must comply with them.
- **Request destination-market certificates** under articles 4 to 6 of the Decree, without proving the foreign regulation.
- **Prepare for discussions at customs**, with a copy of the Decree and a written record of any improper demand.
- **Follow the regulatory calendar:** sector regulations (Decree, art. 8), the Tariff review by COMEX (Decree, art. 7) and the resolution on legalization and apostille (Decree, art. 9).
- **Organize the VAT refund file** from the first shipment.

## The practical takeaway

The Decree changes the question exporters ask. It is no longer whether the State authorizes the sale abroad, but which documents the buyer and the destination country require. For the fishing and coffee sectors, this means the end of prior authorizations and of quotas tied to domestic supply.

Its actual reach will depend on three factors the Decree does not control on its own: that customs offices stop demanding the eliminated Legal Regimes, that sector regulations do not reintroduce the controls, and that COMEX completes the review of the Tariff. And the economic benefit of exporting will remain tied to a timely VAT refund. A well-prepared file from the first shipment is the best guarantee that simplification also reaches cash flow.
