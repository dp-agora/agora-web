Over more than thirty years of legal practice, we have frequently observed a mistake that, while understandable, can have serious consequences: some businessmen —sometimes even the most experienced— confuse their own legal personality with that of the companies they control. This is not a minor oversight. It is a conceptual confusion with deep roots. This article aims to explain, in plain terms, one of the most powerful concepts in law: legal personality.

## What is legal personality?

Legal personality is the capacity of a subject to hold rights and obligations — in other words, to acquire assets, enter into contracts, incur debts, and appear before courts. This attribute is not exclusive to human beings. The law also recognizes it in entities that, although they have no physical existence, do have legal existence: the so-called legal persons or legal entities. Among them are corporations (or stock companies), limited liability companies, incorporated partnerships, foundations, and civil associations, among many other legal forms created to organize economic and social life.

## An institution with more than two thousand years of history

The idea of granting legal personality to entities other than human beings is not a modern invention. Its roots go back, at least, to Roman law.

In Rome, it was recognized that certain groupings —such as municipalities (municipia), religious and funerary colleges (collegia), and associations of craftsmen and traders (corpora)— could act in the legal world independently of their individual members. They could own property, enter into contracts, and be represented before courts. The term Roman jurists used for these collective entities was universitas: an entity distinct from the sum of its members.

This legacy was systematized in the Corpus Juris Civilis, the codification of Roman law commissioned by Emperor Justinian between 529 and 534 AD. Rediscovered in Europe around the 12th century, this work became the foundation of modern civil law and, directly or indirectly, of the civil and commercial codes that govern most countries in Latin America and continental Europe today.

## The company is a separate legal person from its shareholders

In the modern business world, the most common manifestation of legal personality is the corporation. It exists as a legal person independent of its shareholders. The practical consequences of that separation are fundamental:

- Contracts entered into by the company bind the company, not its shareholders.
- Debts incurred by the company are the company's debts, not those of its shareholders.
- Assets belonging to the company do not belong to the shareholders: they are the company's property.
- The company's bank accounts belong to the company, not to its shareholders.

Since a corporation is an entity with no physical existence, it necessarily acts through individuals. For this purpose, the law establishes corporate organs —such as the shareholders' meeting, the board of directors or management committee, and the general manager— composed of individuals. When these individuals act in their capacity as organs of the company, they do so as representatives of the legal entity, and not in a personal capacity. An act performed by a director or officer in the exercise of their functions is, legally, an act of the company: it binds the company, not the individual who carried it out.

A shareholder's liability is, in principle, limited to the amount of its capital contribution. This limitation of liability is one of the most powerful features of the corporate form: it allows business risks to be taken without jeopardizing personal assets.

## The most common mistakes — and their consequences

Despite the above, we have seen people without legal training treat the company as an extension of themselves, especially in start-ups and family companies. The most common mistakes include:

- Using the company's bank accounts as personal accounts, moving funds in and out without contractual support or accounting justification.
- Entering into obligations personally that should be undertaken by the company, or vice versa.
- Conflating the company's assets with the shareholder's personal assets.
- Failing to properly record corporate decisions authorizing acts taken on behalf of the company.

These practices generate opacity and can generate legal, tax, and accounting problems. Moreover, they can have very serious consequences: from difficulties in obtaining financing, making the company fail a substance-over-form analysis for tax and legal purposes, to the shareholder's personal exposure to the company's debts.

## Piercing the corporate veil: the exception that proves the rule

The principle of separation between the company's legal identity and that of its shareholders is solid, but not absolute. When the corporate form is used fraudulently or abusively — to evade taxes, defraud creditors, or circumvent legal obligations — courts may "pierce the corporate veil" and hold shareholders or managers directly liable for the company's debts or conduct.

This doctrine — recognized in virtually all modern legal systems — exists precisely because legal personality is an instrument in the service of law, not a license for fraud. Using it properly protects. Abusing it can result in unlimited personal liability.

**A well-governed company — with clear records, separate accounts, documented decisions, and respect for corporate procedures — is not only better protected legally: it also inspires greater confidence in partners, clients, banks, and regulators. Roman law understood this more than two thousand years ago: recognizing that an entity has its own legal existence is a decision of enormous consequence. So is respecting it.**
