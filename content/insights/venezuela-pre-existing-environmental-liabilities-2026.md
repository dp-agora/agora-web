The Organic Environmental Law, the Environmental Criminal Law, and Decree No. 2,635 draw no distinction between the damage you caused and the damage you found. Whoever operates, answers. In the midst of Venezuela's industrial reactivation, that rule has direct consequences for anyone acquiring assets, leasing plants, or taking over operations that have been idle.

This is the sixth article in a series on Venezuelan Environmental Law applied to investment projects. The previous five covered the [Venezuelan environmental regulatory framework](/insights/venezuela-economic-revival-environmental-regulation), the [Environmental and Sociocultural Impact Assessment (ESIA)](/insights/venezuela-oil-gas-electricity-reactivation-esia-2026), the [Territory Occupation Authorization (AOT)](/insights/venezuela-territory-occupation-authorization-aot-2026), the [role of the registered environmental consultant](/insights/venezuela-environmental-consultants-2026), and the [regime governing hazardous materials and waste](/insights/venezuela-hazardous-waste-regulation-2026) under Decree No. 2,635 and the Law on Hazardous Substances, Materials, and Waste. All reached the same conclusion: environmental assessment is the legal backbone of any project in Venezuela. This article addresses the problem that arises before that assessment begins — the liabilities already in place when the new operator arrives.

## Why This Matters Now

- Article 127 of the Venezuelan Constitution enshrines every person's right to a safe, healthy, and ecologically balanced environment. The duty to protect it falls on private operators, not only on the State.
- The industrial reactivation underway in 2026 means restarting operations that stood idle for years: deteriorated plants, unmaintained storage areas, and soil contaminated by spills that were never remediated. Whoever arrives at a site in that condition inherits the problem.
- A pre-existing environmental liability is not a doctrinal abstraction. It materializes as remediation obligations, administrative penalties, criminal exposure for directors and officers, and contingencies that erode asset value and project viability.

## What Is an Environmental Liability?

The Organic Environmental Law (the "Environmental Law") (Official Gazette No. 5,833 Ext. of December 22, 2006), defines environmental damage as any alteration that adversely affects the functional integrity and carrying capacity of the ecosystems on which species survival and human quality of life depend. An environmental liability is that damage crystallized at a specific site: accumulated, unremedied contamination that persists as a latent obligation attached to the property or the activity.

In practice, environmental liability takes the following forms:

- Soil contaminated by hydrocarbon spills or heavy metals.
- Groundwater or surface water contaminated by untreated historical discharges.
- Hazardous waste accumulated without authorized disposal — the subject of the previous article in this series.
- Deteriorated containment infrastructure, with a risk of contaminant migration.
- Improvised disposal areas that fail to meet Decree No. 2,635 (Official Gazette No. 5,245 Ext. of August 3, 1998).

Each of these conditions triggers a remediation obligation that the law imposes on the site operator, regardless of who caused it.

## The Core Rule: Whoever Operates, Answers

- The Environmental Law establishes strict liability in environmental matters. The duty to repair the damage does not require proof of fault: the damage and its causal link to the activity are enough. The current operator who maintains or fails to remediate a damaging condition is liable even if a prior operator caused it.
- Decree No. 2,635 reinforces the principle: a generator of hazardous waste is not released from liability by hiring a third party to manage it. The same logic applies to anyone taking over an industrial activity with pre-existing liabilities. Transferring the asset does not automatically transfer the originator's liability, nor does it release the new operator from its own management obligations.
- The Environmental Criminal Law (Official Gazette No. 39,913 of May 2, 2012) adds the criminal dimension. Environmental offenses carry prison sentences, and liability reaches those who make operational decisions, not only the legal entity. A director who authorizes start-up at a site with known liabilities, without managing them, is personally exposed.

## Environmental Due Diligence: What to Review Before Closing

Identifying liabilities before operating or closing the transaction is the only effective way to manage the risk. Environmental due diligence is not a process separate from legal analysis: it is an integral part of it. Counsel must run it alongside a consultant registered with the [Ministry of People's Power for Ecosocialism](https://www.minec.gob.ve/) (the "MINEC"), as discussed in the fourth article in this series. The review must cover five fronts:

- **Permit and authorization history.** The project file at the MINEC shows whether the prior operator obtained the required permits, whether it was penalized, and whether enforcement proceedings remain open. Those proceedings do not lapse when the operator changes.
- **Condition of soil and groundwater.** A site assessment detects contamination before the activity is assumed. It is the basic technical tool for quantifying the liability and sizing the remediation obligation.
- **Inventory of hazardous waste and materials.** Under Decree No. 2,635 and the Law on Hazardous Substances, Materials, and Waste (Official Gazette No. 5,554 Ext. of November 13, 2001), any facility that has generated hazardous materials or waste must have them identified, classified, and disposed of in accordance with the law.
- **Compliance with the Water Law.** The Water Law (Official Gazette No. 38,595 of January 2, 2007) requires use concessions and discharge permits. Historical operation without those authorizations creates a regulatory liability that may be enforced against the new operator.
- **Liabilities toward communities.** The Environmental Law grants affected communities the right to demand remediation. If the prior operator harmed neighboring communities, that conflict may resurface when the new operator arrives.

## Implications for Mergers & Acquisitions, Financing, and Contract Structures

From a transactional standpoint, a pre-existing environmental liability is a risk the parties must allocate expressly. Contractual silence does not protect the buyer: Venezuelan law imposes obligations on the current operator regardless of what the purchase or lease agreement says. The same principle extends to financing, since lenders require environmental representations as a condition to disbursement, and an unmanaged liability may trigger events of default under the loan documents.

Five contractual tools are available:

- **Specific representations and warranties.** The seller must state the environmental condition of the asset precisely. A generic compliance representation is not enough: the buyer needs a statement on the absence of known liabilities, the status of permits, the absence of open enforcement proceedings, and compliance with Decree No. 2,635.
- **Specific indemnities.** Where due diligence identifies liabilities, market practice is to negotiate a dedicated indemnity for them, with liability caps, coverage periods, and notice and defense mechanisms.
- **Price retention (escrow or holdback).** A portion of the price is withheld until remediation is evidenced or the administrative proceeding is closed. This protects the buyer against liabilities that could not be quantified during the review.
- **Condition precedent or price adjustment.** If the review reveals liabilities of uncertain magnitude, the buyer may require remediation as a condition precedent to closing or negotiate an adjustment reflecting the estimated cost of managing them.
- **Lease versus acquisition.** An industrial lease may offer greater flexibility in allocating responsibility for pre-existing liabilities. Environmental law may nonetheless impose obligations on the lessee in its capacity as operator, regardless of what the parties agree. The contract must be precise on this point.

## The Practical Conclusion

A pre-existing environmental liability does not disappear when the operator changes, and Venezuelan law offers no contractual way out. Anyone evaluating an opportunity in Venezuela must build environmental due diligence into the structure of the process, not treat it as a post-closing step. Counsel must coordinate with a registered environmental consultant from the analysis phase, not from the operating phase.

The practical rule is straightforward: assess before you acquire, negotiate before you operate. The cost of well-executed environmental due diligence is a fraction of the cost of assuming an unidentified liability. And in the context of Venezuela's industrial reactivation, where many assets have been idle for years, the probability of finding liabilities is high.
