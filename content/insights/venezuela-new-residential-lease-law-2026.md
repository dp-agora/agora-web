The residential lease market in Venezuela has needed a regulatory framework capable of encouraging property rentals for years. Up until now, applicable regulations discouraged formal supply, leaving a large portion of available inventory closed off. That situation became critical following the earthquakes of June 24, 2026, which left thousands of families without housing and put upward pressure on prices.

The Law on the Special Regime for the Lease of Residential Properties responds to that need. Passed by the National Assembly on July 31, 2026, and published in Official Gazette No. 7,065 Ext. of August 7, 2026, it is currently in effect. It's worth noting that it did not repeal the Law for the Regularization and Control of Residential Leases or Decree-Law No. 8,190 against Arbitrary Eviction and Unhousing of Residences, both from 2011: all three instruments coexist in a parallel regime. The applicable law doesn't depend solely on the contract date: Article 2 excludes properties in the tenant's possession as of August 7, 2026, from the special regime for as long as that possession persists. A property enters the new regime only when the current tenant vacates it. Without specialized counsel, there is a risk of signing unbalanced contracts or compromising the recovery of the property in the event of a dispute.

**Key considerations regarding the new law:**

- **State role:** the special regime doesn't contemplate a governing body like the National Superintendency of Residential Leases (SUNAVI) to oversee contract eligibility, registration, official rent setting, inspections, or fines. Nor is there a public office where claims can be filed: claims are contractual and are resolved between the parties, before a judge, or before a mediator, conciliator, and/or arbitrator.
- **Rent and adjustments:** the parties freely agree on the rent amount. During the term of the contract, the rent doesn't vary; it can only be adjusted upon extension or renewal, by mutual agreement. Article 13 caps that adjustment to the variation in the Consumer Price Index of the Central Bank of Venezuela only when the rent is set in Bolívares. If agreed in foreign currency, there is no legal limit, so the adjustment formula must be settled in the contract.
- **Account currency and payment currency:** Article 12 leaves the rent to the free will of the parties, and Article 14 allows it to be established in foreign currency. It is useful to distinguish between the currency of account—in which the obligation is denominated—and the currency of payment—in which it must be fulfilled. Article 128 of the Law of the Central Bank of Venezuela allows foreign currency obligations to be paid in Bolívares according to the exchange rate on the payment date, unless otherwise agreed. Since Article 14 requires mutual agreement for that option, in the absence of an express provision, the foreign currency would be the mandatory payment currency, as Article 14 is a special and subsequent rule. There is an opposing interpretation that attributes a discharging effect to Article 128 unless a special agreement exists. That debate alone is enough reason not to leave the point open. It is a clause that cannot be omitted, and it must specify whether the foreign currency is the currency of account or the exclusive currency of payment.
- **Duration and continuation of the contract:** the term and its extensions are agreed upon by the parties, and in the absence of a stipulation, it is understood to be agreed for one year. If neither party expresses a desire to terminate upon expiration, the contract continues under the same terms. The law does not contemplate a statutory extension nor does it establish a minimum notice period, making contractual regulation advisable. Article 11 of the law also fails to clarify whether that continuation renews the contract for an equal term or converts it into an indefinite-term lease subject to the eviction rules of Articles 1,600 and 1,614 of the Civil Code. Upon that distinction depends whether the landlord remains bound for another full period. And until an adjustment agreement is reached, the rent remains unchanged.
- **Guarantees:** the parties may agree on a security deposit or an insurance policy of up to three months' rent to cover property maintenance and overdue utility payments. Refunds must occur within 15 continuous days following the end of the relationship, deducting any owed repairs and utilities.
- **Early termination and eviction:** Article 23 sets specific grounds, including non-payment of rent for three consecutive periods and non-payment of utilities for three consecutive months. Eviction based on the owner's personal need is not contemplated. Furthermore, the prior administrative process before SUNAVI disappears, and, per the Sole Transitional Provision, the enforcement guarantees of Decree-Law No. 8,190 do not apply: the suspension of 90 to 180 business days, the prior provision of shelter, and the 90-day notice period. This represents the greatest time savings for the landlord and, at the same time, the flank most vulnerable to a challenge based on retrogressiveness. The most sensitive provision is the possibility of incorporating additional grounds for eviction into the contract, as the non-waivable nature of tenant rights is not established. On the other hand, the tenant remains protected against arbitrary eviction: the order can only come from a municipal court or an arbitral tribunal, pursuant to Article 27. When contained in an arbitral award, it must be submitted to the competent municipal court for enforcement.
- **Unilateral termination by the tenant:** Article 24 allows the tenant to terminate the contract at any time. The parties may agree on an indemnity capped at two months' rent. Anyone projecting rental income must account for this risk from day one.
- **Right of first refusal:** Article 20 recognizes a solvent tenant with more than two years of possession as having the right to be offered the property for sale before a third party, with 15 days to respond. If the owner lowers the price or improves the conditions, it must be offered again. The law does not establish legal redemption (*retracto legal*), so the preference is not enforceable against third-party purchasers, limiting the tenant to claiming damages.
- **Repairs, utilities, and expenses:** in the absence of an agreement, the landlord assumes repairs exceeding 30% of the monthly rent, while the tenant assumes those that do not; utilities are borne by the tenant, while the condo fee and urban property tax are borne by the landlord. All of these rules permit agreement to the contrary, which is why it is advisable to review them one by one.
- **Dispute resolution:** Article 25 explicitly opens space for alternative dispute resolution mechanisms: conciliation, mediation, and arbitration, whether before institutional centers, agreed upon by the parties, or through arbitration under the Code of Civil Procedure; municipal courts are also competent via summary proceedings. The path will depend on what is stipulated in the contract. This is an important step: private arbitration centers offer swift, specialized procedures at reasonable costs, making them attractive for these types of disputes. It should be noted that Article 26 states the State may create mediation and arbitration centers, which has not yet occurred: for now, private centers remain the primary viable option.
- **Authentication:** It is advisable to authenticate the contract, as the new law does not regulate buyer subrogation or the death of the tenant, and Article 1,604 of the Civil Code only obligates a purchaser to respect a lease that is documented with a certified date.

Venezuela has five legal instruments regarding property leases, in addition to the provisions of the Civil Code and the Commercial Code: the Decree with Rank and Force of Law on Real Estate Leases (1999), the Law for the Regularization and Control of Residential Leases (2011), the Decree with Rank, Value, and Force of Law against Arbitrary Eviction and Unhousing of Residences (2011), the Decree with Rank, Value, and Force of Law on the Regulation of Commercial Real Estate Leases (2014), and the Law on the Special Regime for the Lease of Residential Properties (2026), each with its own scope of application. Determining which one applies and reviewing the critical aspects of the contract, the obligations of each party, and the specific position as landlord or tenant is best done with professional assistance before signing.
