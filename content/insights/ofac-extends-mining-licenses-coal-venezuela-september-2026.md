## At a Glance

- **What changed.** As of September 2, 2026, the three general licenses covering Venezuela's mining sector cover coal. And they name Carbones del Zulia, S.A. alongside Minerven as a state counterparty.
- **What remains.** Everything else. The dispute resolution forum in the United States, the United Kingdom, France or Singapore, the channeling of payments into the Foreign Government Deposit Funds, the exclusions and the reporting stay as they were.
- **Why it matters.** Coal enters the perimeter under the same rules as gold, including the ban on mining in Venezuela without separate OFAC authorization.
- **What to review.** The line between servicing a coal mine, which GL 54C allows, and operating it, which no general license allows.

![Infographic: OFAC adds coal to its Venezuela mining licenses — GL 51D, GL 54C, and GL 55A (September 2, 2026)](/images/insights/ofac-september-2026-coal-mining-infographic.html)

On September 2, 2026, the Office of Foreign Assets Control ("OFAC") of the U.S. Department of the Treasury [amended three licenses](https://ofac.treasury.gov/recent-actions/20260902) under the Venezuela program and the [Venezuela Sanctions Regulations](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-V/part-591) (31 CFR part 591). [GL 51D](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/936856/download) replaces GL 51C, [GL 54C](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/936861/download) replaces GL 54B, and [GL 55A](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/936866/download) replaces GL 55. Each one supersedes its prior version in its entirety as of that same date.

The amendment does one thing. Where the licenses said "minerals, including gold," they now say "coal or minerals, including gold." And where they named CVG Compañía General de Minería de Venezuela CA ("Minerven"), they now also name Carbones del Zulia, S.A. ("Carbozulia"). So far as our review of the text reaches, there is no other substantive change.

The action continues the sequence OFAC has deployed throughout 2026, which we have analyzed in previous installments:

- **March.** Four licenses defined the sectoral scope of the new framework, which covered hydrocarbons, petrochemicals, electricity and minerals (see José P. Barnola Jr., [Four New OFAC General Licenses: The New Framework for Doing Business with Venezuela](/insights/ofac-general-licenses-venezuela-march-2026)).
- **April.** Two licenses enabled contingent contracts and payment channels (see José P. Barnola Jr. and Raúl Sancristóbal, [Two New OFAC General Licenses: Contingent Contracts and Financial Services with Venezuela](/insights/ofac-general-licenses-venezuela-april-2026)).
- **May.** One license opened the advisory layer for a potential debt restructuring (see José P. Barnola Jr. and Raúl Sancristóbal, [OFAC's New General License: Advisory Services for a Potential Restructuring of Venezuelan Debt](/insights/ofac-general-license-58-venezuela-debt-restructuring-advisory-services)).
- **June 10.** Seven energy and mining licenses standardized their contractual clauses (see José P. Barnola Jr. and Raúl Sancristóbal, [OFAC Amended Seven Venezuela General Licenses and Standardized Their Contractual Framework](/insights/ofac-amended-seven-venezuela-general-licenses-june-2026)).
- **August 27.** OFAC removed the U.S.-law requirement from eight licenses (see José P. Barnola Jr. and Raúl Sancristóbal, [OFAC Removes the U.S.-Law Requirement from Eight Venezuela General Licenses](/insights/ofac-removes-us-law-requirement-venezuela-general-licenses-august-2026)).
- **September 2.** Six days later, GLs 51C and 54B change again, this time to admit coal. GL 55 changes for the first time since it was issued in March.

## Three Licenses, Three Stages of the Business

The three licenses work as a system. Each one covers a different stage of the coal business.

GL 51D covers trade. It authorizes an established U.S. entity to buy, sell, store, transport and export Venezuelan coal. The license defines an established U.S. entity as one organized under the laws of the United States, or of any jurisdiction within it, on or before January 29, 2025. The authorization also reaches the processing or refining of the coal, provided it does not take place in Venezuela or in Russia, Iran, North Korea, Cuba or China.

GL 54C covers supply. It authorizes the provision, from the United States or by U.S. persons, of goods, technology, software and services to explore, develop, extract, process or produce coal in Venezuela. It includes the maintenance of operations and the repair of equipment. Unlike GL 51D, it does not require the supplier to be an established U.S. entity.

GL 55A covers investment. It authorizes the negotiation of and entry into contingent contracts for new investment in the sector, whether exploration, the expansion of existing operations or new joint ventures. The contract may be performed only once OFAC authorizes it separately.

## Coal and Carbozulia Enter the Licenses

OFAC did not explain why it added coal. We believe it sought to settle a doubt. Coal is a fossil fuel of organic origin, and the word "minerals" the licenses used did not clearly cover it.

The reference to Carbozulia has a more concrete effect. The licenses already covered transactions with the Government of Venezuela, which Executive Order 13884 defines to include any person it owns or controls, directly or indirectly. Naming Carbozulia identifies the natural counterparty for Venezuelan coal and subjects it, by name, to the same conditions as Minerven. Every contract with Carbozulia must submit its disputes to the United States, the United Kingdom, France or Singapore. And every payment to Carbozulia, other than local taxes, permits and fees, must go into the Foreign Government Deposit Funds under [Executive Order 14373](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/01/15/2026-00831/safeguarding-venezuelan-oil-revenue-for-the-good-of-the-american-and-venezuelan-people).

One drafting detail. For Minerven, the licenses extend the reference to every entity in which it holds 50 percent or more. For Carbozulia they do not. In our understanding, its subsidiaries remain covered as part of the Government of Venezuela, even though the license does not name them.

## What OFAC Said in Its FAQs

[FAQ 1247](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/1247), amended on that same September 2, extends to coal the safe harbor for non-U.S. persons. A buyer from another country may import Venezuelan coal without sanctions exposure if it meets the mirror conditions of GL 51D. Payments to blocked persons must go into the Foreign Government Deposit Funds. And the coal may not be processed or refined in Russia, Iran, North Korea, Cuba or China.

The FAQ warns that anyone operating outside those conditions risks being designated. Among the grounds, it lists operating in the gold or oil sectors, but it does not mention coal. We do not read that omission as an open door. In our view, the risk rests on the other two grounds the FAQ names, support for blocked persons and deceptive practices or corruption involving the Government of Venezuela. FAQs are non-binding interpretive guidance.

## Reading It Through Venezuelan Law

Under Venezuelan law, coal has always been a mineral. The Organic Mining Law (Official Gazette No. 7,020 Ext. of April 16, 2026) does not name it, but it defines a mineral resource as natural material that is "inorganic or fossilized organic." And it divides mining into branches that include "energy mining."

The law matters more for the routes it offers an investor. The State is the sole owner of mineral deposits. A private party may participate in several ways, and two of them run through a company such as Carbozulia. It may sign an operations contract with the state company that holds the mining right, which requires the authorization of the Minister of Popular Power for Ecological Mining Development and Basic Industries and, for large-scale projects, review by the Office of the Attorney General of the Republic. Or it may partner with the State in a mixed company, whose creation requires a presidential decree issued in the Council of Ministers.

Each route fits the licenses differently. The mixed company is a new joint venture in Venezuela. Neither GL 51D nor GL 54C authorizes it, and GL 55A only allows it to be negotiated and signed, with performance subject to separate OFAC authorization. The operations contract sits in a gray area. GL 54C authorizes services "for" the extraction of coal, which would allow it to be framed as a service to Carbozulia. But under that contract the private party extracts the coal itself, an activity that GL 51D leaves outside its authorization and that GL 55A treats as new investment. OFAC has not clarified which of the two categories it falls into.

## What Does It Mean in Practice?

**For the U.S. buyer or trader:** GL 51D allows coal to be bought from Carbozulia today. The burden lies in reporting. OFAC applies to coal the most demanding standard in the program, designed for gold. The first report is due 10 days after the first transaction and the following ones every 30 days. Each one must include the due diligence documentation on the chain of custody. It is worth assembling that file before the first shipment.

**For the equipment and service provider:** GL 54C covers machinery, spare parts, technical assistance and maintenance. Reports are due every 90 days. Before signing, it is worth measuring the scope of the service. Maintaining a plant or supplying equipment falls clearly within the license. Operating the mine does not. Nor, in our view, does a scheme that pays the supplier in tons of coal instead of money, because whoever receives and sells that coal would be operating under GL 51D, which excludes in-kind payments.

**For the investor:** GL 55A allows due diligence, negotiation and signing. But the investment needs two authorizations that run on separate tracks. OFAC must authorize performance separately, whether through a specific license or a future general license. And the Venezuelan State must issue the mixed-company decree or authorize the operations contract. We consider it prudent for the contract to make its effectiveness contingent on both.

**For non-U.S. counterparties:** the FAQ 1247 safe harbor already covers coal.

## What Did Not Change

The rest of the scaffolding remains intact. All three licenses keep the exclusions for Russia, Iran, North Korea and Cuba. GL 51D also excludes Venezuelan or U.S. entities owned or controlled by persons from China, and GLs 54C and 55A directly exclude persons located in China.

The unblocking of property and transactions involving blocked vessels remain excluded. Reports under GLs 51D and 54C still go to the Department of State and the Department of the Interior.

And, as with every general license in the program, OFAC retains the authority to revoke, amend or restrict them at any time.

## The Strategic Takeaway

Three signals emerge from this amendment. The first is that OFAC expands by product, not by sector. It did not create a coal license. It inserted coal and Carbozulia into an architecture that already existed, with its conditions, exclusions and reporting. Any mineral OFAC decides to open in the future, we estimate, will come in through the same door.

The second is that each opening comes with a named counterparty, Minerven for gold and Carbozulia for coal. OFAC opens the product and keeps control over who gets paid, where disputes are heard and where the money goes.

The third is speed. GL 51C was in force for six days. Anyone operating under these licenses must check the current version before each transaction, and their compliance file must cite GL 51D, not its predecessor.

None of this amounts to a lifting of sanctions. The general blocking of the Government of Venezuela under Executive Order 13884 remains in place. These licenses are windows within it, and they keep their exclusions, their reporting and the financial control of the Foreign Government Deposit Funds.

For any company considering entering Venezuelan coal, the recommendation is the usual one. Obtain specialized legal advice before signing, and in this case before deciding whether your contract is a service or an operation.
