When a businessman assesses the tax burden of its operations in Venezuela, the first thought is usually the income tax ("Income Tax"), the value-added tax ("VAT"), the municipal real estate tax, the municipal tax on economic activities and, for importers, import duties and fees. That view is understandable, but incomplete. The Venezuelan system also imposes a set of special contributions and social security contributions that apply to a company simply for generating revenue above certain thresholds or for having employees. These contributions are calculated on different bases, filed with different tax administrations and, taken together, can represent a significant portion of the total tax burden.

In our practice we have seen that some companies lack clarity on the full scope of these obligations. The result is typically the same: defaults by omission, unexpected assessments and remediation costs that could have been avoided with a proper initial mapping. This article offers, in a single piece, a panoramic view of the main special contributions currently in force in Venezuela and their essential elements.

## What are special contributions and social security contributions, and why do they matter?

Special contributions are taxes whose triggering event is the obtaining by the taxpayer of benefits or increases in the value of its property, arising from the performance of public works or state activities, the proceeds of which must be allocated to financing such actions. Social security contributions are levies on employers and employees that are members of the beneficiary groups, intended to finance social welfare services. In practice, both categories of contributions translate into mandatory contributions payable to autonomous agencies such as the National Integrated Service of Customs and Tax Administration ("SENIAT"), the Venezuelan Social Security Institute ("IVSS"), the National Institute of Socialist Educational Cooperation ("INCES"), the National Housing and Habitat Bank ("BANAVIH"), the National Fund for Science and Technology ("FONACIT"), the National Sports Institute ("IND") and the National Anti-Drug Office ("ONA"), among others.

This institutional dispersion has relevant practical consequences:

- Each collecting agency sets its own filing calendar, its own forms and its own audit practices.
- The audit and penalty regime is governed, as a general rule, by the Organic Tax Code.
- The deduction of the contributions for Income Tax purposes is not uniform: most are deductible, but the sports contribution is not, by express legal mandate.

A relevant data point: the case law of the Supreme Tribunal of Justice has held that contributions to the Mandatory Housing Savings Fund ("FAOV") are not tax in nature, but rather social security contributions linked to housing savings. The practical consequence of that characterization is procedural: BANAVIH assessments are not litigated before the tax court jurisdiction, but before the ordinary administrative court.

## 1. Special contributions linked to economic activity

The first block is triggered by the mere conduct of economic activity above certain revenue or profit thresholds, regardless of the size of the payroll.

**Sectoral exemptions for hydrocarbons and mining.** The 2026 reforms of the Organic Hydrocarbons Law and the Organic Mining Law exempt persons engaged in the activities regulated by those laws from paying the following special contributions: science and technology, sports, anti-drugs and protection of pensions. Social security contributions are not within the scope of this exemption. The exemptions apply only to persons that directly execute the activities regulated under each Law; suppliers and contractors remain subject to the general regime. These laws also exempt those sectors from the high net worth tax and from state and municipal taxes, and declare inapplicable the social responsibility commitment under the Public Procurement Law.

### 1.1. Science, technology and innovation contribution

The contribution to FONACIT underwent a transformation with the 2022 reform to the Organic Law of Science and Technology. Two regimes should be distinguished.

**Until 2022:** Paid by legal entities with annual gross income exceeding 100,000 Tax Units. The filing was annual, on the gross income of the fiscal year.

**From 2023:** Paid by legal entities with annual gross income exceeding 150,000 times the higher-value exchange rate. The filing became monthly, on the prior month's gross income.

This change has three practical implications:

- The threshold, previously anchored to the Tax Unit—which the SENIAT has kept without significant adjustment for long periods—, is now updated daily with the exchange rate. In practice, it is much closer to the taxpayer's economic reality.
- The calculation ceased to be an annual exercise and was incorporated into the monthly compliance cycle.
- Rates vary by activity:
- Gambling, games of chance and alcohol: 2%.
- Other activities: 0.5%.

The contribution is deductible for Income Tax purposes.

### 1.2. Sports contribution

The sports contribution, administered by the IND, applies to for-profit companies and other public and private organizations whose annual net profit or accounting profit exceeds 20,000 Tax Units. The filing is annual.

- Taxable base: accounting net profit.
- Rate: 1%.

Two features distinguish the sports contribution from the rest of the catalogue. First, its base is net profit—not gross income or salary—, which makes it similar to an additional Income Tax. Second, the Organic Law of Sports, Physical Activity and Physical Education itself states that the contribution is not deductible for Income Tax purposes. The economic effect is greater than it appears: the nominal 1% rate on net profit translates into a higher effective burden because, by not being deductible, it increases the Income Tax base of the same fiscal year.

### 1.3. Anti-drug contribution

The anti-drug contribution, administered by the ONA, applies to private legal entities, consortia and public entities with business purposes employing 50 or more workers. The filing is annual.

- Taxable base: operating profit or earnings.
- Rate: 1%.

One specific aspect deserves attention: for the computation of the 50-worker threshold, the Organic Drug Law applies the group consolidation rule. If a company is part of an economic group, the employees of all related entities are aggregated to determine whether the threshold is reached. In practice, this means that a company with 40 employees is not automatically out of scope if it has a related company with 15 additional employees: both companies are subject. The contribution is deductible for Income Tax purposes.

## 2. Social security contributions

The second block of contributions is triggered by the existence of employees. Five contributions make up this block.

### 2.1. Social Security

The social security contribution to the IVSS applies to both employers and employees. The employer files monthly and withholds the employee's contribution, although the calculation is made weekly. The taxable base is the normal salary, capped at five minimum wages. The rates vary depending on the risk level of the activity:

- Employer: 9%, 10% or 11% (typically 10%).
- Employee: 4%, 5% or 6% (typically 4%).

The practical formula is: ([monthly salary × 12 / 52] × rate) × number of Mondays in the month (four or five). The contribution is deductible for Income Tax purposes.

### 2.2. Unemployment Insurance Regime

The contribution to the Unemployment Insurance Regime, formerly known as "Paro Forzoso", is also administered by the IVSS. Employers and employees contribute monthly on the basis of the normal salary, capped at 10 minimum wages. The employer withholds the employee's contribution:

- Employer: 2%.
- Employee: 0.5%.

The calculation formula is the same as for Social Security. The contribution is deductible for Income Tax purposes.

### 2.3. INCES

The contribution to the INCES applies to employers with more than five employees, and to those employees. The employer files quarterly; the employer withholds the employee's contribution at the time of the annual payment of profit-sharing.

- Employer: 2% on the normal salary.
- Employee: 0.5% on profit-sharing.

The contribution is deductible for Income Tax purposes. In practice, it is advisable to provision for the cost of the contribution on profit-sharing within the annual budget, since the withholding concentrates in the month profit-sharing is paid (typically December) and may generate a meaningful cash mismatch.

### 2.4. FAOV

The contribution to the FAOV, administered by the BANAVIH, levies on the monthly payroll, and the employer withholds the employee's contribution. The taxable base is the integral salary.

- Employer: 2%.
- Employee: 1%.

As noted above, according to case law, this contribution is not a tax in the strict sense. That characterization is relevant, among other things, for procedural and statute-of-limitations purposes. The contribution is deductible for Income Tax purposes.

### 2.5. Contribution for the Protection of Pensions

The contribution for the protection of pensions is the most recent addition to the catalogue, created in 2024. It is administered by the SENIAT—not the IVSS—, which makes it, in practical terms, a special contribution with management peculiarities.

- Taxpayers: legal entities and partnerships, including irregular or de facto partnerships, of a private character, whether or not domiciled in Venezuela, that conduct economic activities in the country, employ workers and pay salaries and non-salary bonuses.
- Taxable base: salaries and non-salary bonuses.
- Minimum threshold: indexed minimum integral income.
- Rate: 9%.
- Period: monthly filing, in accordance with the annual calendar published by the SENIAT.

The contribution is deductible for Income Tax purposes. The breadth of the taxable base—which covers both salaries and non-salary bonuses (food allowance, special vacation bonuses and others)—requires careful identification of the items subject to the contribution.

## 4. Practical considerations

Several considerations follow from the foregoing that, in our experience, should be incorporated into the tax and operational design of any company operating in Venezuela.

**Initial mapping.** Before commencing operations, or when updating the tax structure of an existing company, it is advisable to map all contributions applicable to the business profile. The most common error we see is the retroactive surprise: a contribution the taxpayer was unaware of which, upon audit, generates assessments with default interest and fines.

**Integrated calendar.** Each tax authority has its own calendar. The IVSS, INCES and BANAVIH use monthly or quarterly cycles. FONACIT moved to monthly in 2023. The IND and ONA are annual. The pensions contribution adds a monthly SENIAT calendar. We have found that an integrated calendar, with assigned responsibilities and alerts, significantly reduces the risk of non-compliance.

**Correct identification of the taxable base.** Taxable bases are not interchangeable: normal salary, integral salary, net profit, operating profit, prior month's gross income, non-salary bonuses. Each concept has a specific legal definition. Applying the wrong base—typically, normal salary instead of integral salary, or vice versa—generates material differences in the amount of the contribution.

**Income Tax deductibility.** Except for the sports contribution, the contributions described here are deductible for Income Tax purposes. Adequate documentary support should be maintained to sustain the deduction in an audit.

## 5. Conclusion

Income Tax, VAT, the municipal real estate tax, the municipal tax on economic activities and import duties remain the most visible taxes in the Venezuelan system, but they do not exhaust the effective tax burden. The contributions described here apply, in the aggregate, to payroll, profits and gross income. Their correct identification, quantification and filing is not an administrative detail: it is a structural component of compliance.

The best antidote to surprise assessments is the same we have been recommending to our clients for years: build a complete initial map, integrate it into a single calendar, assign responsibilities and review it periodically.

Knowing how many special contributions your company pays is the first step to avoid paying twice: once to the collecting agency and a second time, larger, when curing the non-compliance.
